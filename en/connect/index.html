Two Questions, One Meeting Point

The first collection on this site asks whether any part of the Land of Israel may be handed over. The second asks whether a Jew may take part in the Knesset elections. These are separate questions, answered by two different groups of rabbis. No rabbi appears in both collections, and none of them should be credited with a position on a question he did not answer.

Even so, the questions meet at one point: the voter himself.

The voter sends an agent

The central argument of the Zichron BaSefer rabbis is agency. A representative acts in the name of those who chose him, and a person’s agent is like himself. So whoever sends a representative to the Knesset becomes a partner in what is done there:

…Whoever takes part in the elections to choose members who sit in the Knesset — who are the representatives of the voters — automatically becomes a partner and accessory to all the severe transgressions committed there.

Rabbi Hillel Lichtenstein, Av 5721 (1961)

…All the voters become partners, through their representatives sitting in the Knesset, in acknowledging idolatry and in the severe prohibitions.

Rabbi Avraham Yitzchok Kahn, Cheshvan 5749 (1988)

Some will answer that there is no agency for a transgression. But these rabbis did not rest on agency alone. They wrote that the voter himself acknowledges the regime and gives it strength, and that is his own act, not his agent’s:

Whoever takes part in these elections breaches the fence. It is as though he acknowledges the laws of the State, which are against the laws of the Torah, and he gives strength to the Other Side and to those who blaspheme G-d's name.

Rabbi Yaakov Mutzafi, 14 Tammuz 5711 (1951)

And the Knesset decides about the Land

The rabbis of Gedolei Torah al Hachzarat Shetachim ruled that no part of the Land of Israel may be handed over, and that no government has the authority to do it:

The government has no authority to return even a single foot of land.

Rishon LeZion Rabbi Yitzhak Nissim, p. 75

A government that hands over land draws its power from the Knesset, and the Knesset draws its power from the voters. Whoever accepts both rulings together, that handing over the Land is forbidden and that the voter is a partner in what his representatives do, cannot treat his vote as a private matter. That is the conclusion of a reader who sets the two collections side by side, not the words of any one rabbi.
